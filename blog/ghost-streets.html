Ghost Streets: What Reflections Do to Point Clouds

A point cloud may show a second street where there should only be a building. Here is why reflective surfaces can create misleading geometry.

By Esmira Ibrahimli · October 2026 · Technical note

The street that isn't there

While inspecting mobile LiDAR data, you may notice unexpected returns behind a smooth building facade: a duplicate vehicle, road markings, or even a street-like pattern. These are sometimes called ghost points or reflection artifacts. Although their coordinates look plausible, they do not represent physical objects at those positions.

Glass is especially interesting because it can transmit, reflect, and redirect laser energy. Some LiDAR returns can therefore be assigned a misleading location when the processing assumes that the measured light traveled directly to a surface and back.

Why reflections produce ghost geometry

For a simple flat reflective surface, a virtual copy of a scene may appear behind the reflecting plane. A useful way to visualize this is as a mirror image: corresponding features lie across the plane, although real LiDAR reflection paths and reconstruction effects can be more complicated.

The exact artifact depends on the sensor, incidence angle, surface properties, multiple reflections, and software handling of returns. Not every reflective surface creates a clean mirrored road.

How to recognize the artifact

Compare suspicious returns with camera imagery and the surrounding 3D geometry. Look for duplicated shapes across a planar facade, improbable objects inside buildings, and returns that change with the scanning viewpoint. A cross-section or top view can make the apparent symmetry easier to inspect.

Why it matters for mapping

Ghost returns can mislead manual annotation, create false obstacles, and affect automated extraction of curbs, lanes, and other mapped features. The practical lesson is to validate geometry against multiple views and contextual evidence before accepting every point as a real surface.
